How to build a skincare routine that actually works

By Anna H. Chacon, MD, Fellow of the American Academy of Dermatology·May 19, 2026·6 min

Most skincare routines fail for the same reason: they're built around products people heard about, not the skin they actually have. The good news is that a real, effective routine has only four moving parts. Once you understand what each one is for, picking the right product gets dramatically simpler.

This guide walks through the four pillars every routine needs, the difference between morning and evening steps, how to layer without pilling or stinging, and how to introduce a new active ingredient without wrecking your skin. The embedded scanner above can tell you which specific ingredients to look for — each one links to its full entry in our skincare ingredient glossary.

1. The four pillars every routine needs

You only need four kinds of products. Every other label — "essence," "ampoule," "boosting concentrate" — is a marketing variation on one of these four jobs.

  • Cleanser. Removes oil, sunscreen, makeup, and pollution. Choose by skin type, not by claim: gel or foam for oily/combination skin, cream or balm for dry or sensitive skin.
  • Treatment. A serum or leave-on with one or two active ingredients chosen to address your top concerns — niacinamide for redness and pores, vitamin C for brightness, retinol for fine lines.
  • Moisturizer. Holds water in and supports the skin barrier. If your skin feels tight, reactive, or stings when you apply anything, look for ceramides on the label.
  • Sunscreen. Non-negotiable, every morning, rain or shine. Mineral SPF with zinc oxide is the safest pick for reactive or pigmentation-prone skin.

That's it. A four-step routine done consistently outperforms a ten-step routine done sporadically.

The biggest mistake I see in patients' routines isn't using the wrong product — it's using too many at once and never giving any of them long enough to actually work.

— Dr. Anna H. Chacon, MD, FAAD

2. Morning vs. evening — different jobs

Your morning routine has one main job: protect. Your skin spends the day exposed to UV, pollution, and friction. So the morning lineup leans toward antioxidants and sunscreen.

Your evening routine has the opposite job: repair. That's when actives like retinoids and exfoliating acids do their work, your skin makes new collagen, and your barrier rebuilds. Save the harder-working ingredients for night.

  • AM: gentle cleanser → vitamin C serum → hyaluronic acid (optional) → moisturizer → SPF 30+.
  • PM: cleanser (double-cleanse if you wore sunscreen or makeup) → retinol or glycolic acid (not both same night) → ceramide moisturizer.

3. Layering order: thin before thick, water before oil

The rule is simple: apply your products in order of consistency, thinnest to thickest. Water-based serums go on first, then any oil-based serums, then moisturizer, then sunscreen as the final morning step.

Two practical exceptions to know about:

  • Hyaluronic acid goes on damp skin. Hyaluronic acid pulls water — apply it to skin that's still damp from cleansing, or it can pull moisture out instead of in.
  • Retinoids go on dry skin, with a buffer. Wait 5–10 minutes after cleansing so skin is fully dry. Some people apply moisturizer first, then retinoid, to reduce irritation — both approaches work; pick the one your skin tolerates.

4. How to introduce a new active without wrecking your skin

The single biggest mistake in skincare is starting three new actives at once. When something stings, peels, or breaks you out, you have no idea which product is responsible — so you quit them all and lose any progress.

The fix: one new product at a time, and give it 2–4 weeks before adding the next. For strong actives like retinoids and exfoliating acids, start with the lowest frequency the product recommends and build up.

  • Retinol: 2 nights a week for the first two weeks, then 3 nights, then every other night. Most people don't need it nightly.
  • Glycolic acid or salicylic acid: 1–2 nights a week to start. Never on the same night as retinol.
  • Adapalene: expect a 4–6 week purge phase before improvement. Push through with a good moisturizer.

04 — Tone Analysis

Skin tone, scientifically classified

Tone is reported as a Fitzpatrick skin type — the I–VI scale dermatologists use to describe how skin responds to UV — plus the underlying ITA angle (the colorimetric measurement that derives the Fitzpatrick classification).

Why this matters: SPF needs, hyperpigmentation risk, and how well certain actives are tolerated all vary by skin type.

Importantly, Fitzpatrick is descriptive, not a quality measure. We use your classification to tailor product recommendations to skin that behaves like yours — not to rank skin against any other skin.
